# The Great Stink — original script v2 (2026-11-08)

Facts: Hansard HC Deb 15 Jul 1858 (Disraeli, "Stygian pool", £3m borrowing power repaid by a 3d rate over 40 yrs); royal assent 2 Aug 1858 = 18 days; Faraday 1855 "a real sewer"; Godwin "more than six feet deep"; ~2.5-3m population, ~200,000 cesspits, ~360 sewers (1856); lime chloride on curtains + 200-250 long tons of lime at £1,500/week; NO cholera outbreak in London in 1858 (outbreaks: 1831-32, 1848-49, 1853-54, 1866); miasma theory (Chadwick: "all smell is disease"); Bazalgette appointed MBW chief engineer 1856 (not hired in 1858), 82 miles intercepting + ~1,100 miles street sewers, 318m bricks, Portland cement, 1859-75; Metropolis Water Act 1852 forced intakes above Teddington Lock from 31 Aug 1855 — BEFORE the sewers, so by 1858 the drinking supply had already been moved. v2 corrections after fact-check: the closing causal claim is no longer backwards (the sewers carried filth, not the drinking water); "no cholera outbreak that summer" replaces "killed almost nobody"; cesspits named; "borrow" not "approve"; 1,100 miles of street sewers included.

You are standing inside the British Parliament, and you cannot breathe. It is July 1858, the windows are open, and the air coming through them tastes like a sewer. Because it is one. London has two and a half million people, and their waste, from cesspits and drains, empties into a single river. The Thames. It is not water anymore. It is untreated sewage, cooking in the summer heat, and Parliament was built directly beside it. Members soak the curtains in lime, hold handkerchiefs over their faces, and discuss abandoning London for St Albans. The newspapers called it the Great Stink, strong enough to empty a debating chamber. Meanwhile, London had been drinking from that same river, which is why cholera killed tens of thousands of people over the previous twenty years. Then, after decades of refusing to pay, they do something extraordinary. They borrow three million pounds in eighteen days. They hire an engineer called Joseph Bazalgette to dig eighty two miles of brick tunnels beneath the city, feeding eleven hundred miles of street sewers. Now here is the part that makes no sense. They believed the smell itself was the poison. It was not. The foul air was not spreading disease, and there was no cholera outbreak that summer. But the sewers worked anyway, because they carried the filth out of the city, and the smell with it. The cholera had already been beaten by something less dramatic: three years earlier, the water companies moved their intakes upstream. London was saved by a mistake.
